Srimad Bhagavata Kathasagaram

Vāmana Avataram

Mahābali, grandson of the great Prahlāda, ruled the three worlds with a strength, wisdom, and generosity unmatched among the Daityas. Unlike many before him, he did not conquer out of hatred for the Devas nor revel in cruelty; his devotion to dharma was steadfast and his charity boundless. Under the guidance of his guru Shukrāchārya, Bali performed a yajna of such immense potency that the heavens themselves fell under his dominion, and the throne of Indra stood empty.

But the seat of Svarga was not meant for a Daitya, and balance had to be restored.

The exiled Devas turned to Sriman Nārāyaṇa, who had already foreseen all that was to come. He did not regard Bali as an enemy but as a devotee whose greatness would be immortalized through an act of surrender — the heavens would be reclaimed not by war nor deception, but by a lesson that even the mightiest must bow before the Supreme. Aditi, mother of the Devas, distressed by her children's plight, undertook with her husband Kāshyapa the sacred Payovrata, a penance of a milk-based diet observed over twelve days.

Pleased with her devotion, the Lord assured her that He Himself would descend as her son.

And so, on the auspicious Shravana Dvādashī, the Supreme took form as Vāmana, a dwarf brahmachari. As soon as He was born He revealed to Aditi the stages of His life and grew into a young boy, and His upanayanam was performed. The Devas came forward to adorn Him with gifts: Brahmā gave a water pot, Shiva a staff of kusha grass, Sarasvatī a rudrāksha garland, Kubera a begging bowl, Lakshmī robes of yellow silk, Indra a celestial umbrella, the Saptarishis a deer skin, and Sūrya a pair of wooden sandals.

Thus adorned, the radiant Vāmana set out for the sacrificial hall of Mahābali.

Bali, preparing another grand yajna, had declared that none would leave his court emptyhanded. When the young brahmachari entered, the brilliance of His form filled the hall, and Bali rose to receive Him, offering gold, land, cattle — whatever He desired. But Vāmana asked only for three paces of land, measured by His own feet. A murmur of amusement passed through the assembly; what was three steps of land to a king who ruled all three worlds?

Bali urged Him to ask for more, but the boy answered that one who is not content with three steps would not be content even with the whole universe.

At that moment Shukrāchārya, recognizing the Lord, warned Bali that this was Vishṇu Himself, come in the guise of a boy, and that granting His request would cost him everything. But Bali would not waver — if it was indeed the Lord who had come to him, what greater fortune could there be than to give Him all?

Determined to prevent the gift, Shukrāchārya shrank himself and lodged in the spout of the water vessel to block the sealing pour; the Lord, ever playful, took a blade of grass and pushed it into the spout, piercing the sage's eye and driving him out. Even so, Bali kept his word and poured the sacred water, sealing his vow.

Then the small frame of the brahmachari began to expand — across the earth, across the heavens, beyond the cosmos itself. With the first step He measured all of Bhu-Lokam, with the second He reclaimed Svarga, and then He asked Bali where He should place the third. His heart full of devotion, Bali bowed his head and asked the Lord to set the third step upon it. And so the Lord, now in His towering Trivikrama form, placed His foot upon Bali's head and pressed him gently into Patala-Lokam. Yet Bali had not lost.

For his surrender the Lord granted him dominion over Sutala, a realm more resplendent than Svarga, and vowed to stand guard at his gates for all time. Bali had conquered the three worlds, yet his greatest victory lay not in conquest but in surrender — for true victory is not in holding onto power, but in offering everything to the One who is the source of all power.
