Srimad Bhagavata Kathasagaram

Dhruva Charitram

Dhruva was still a child when he left the comforts of a palace in search of something far greater than a kingdom. He was the son of King Uttanapada and Queen Sunīti, yet in his father's court he was made to feel that he did not belong, for the king's other wife, Suruchi, was the favored queen, and it was her son Uttama whom the king openly cherished.

One day Dhruva saw Uttama seated upon his father's lap and ran toward the throne, longing for the same affection. But before he could climb up, Suruchi drew him away and set her own son in his place. She told the boy that he had no right to that seat, that he was born of Sunīti and so was destined for no greatness, and that if he wished for such a place he would have to be born again from her own womb. She warned him never to approach his father in this way again, and the king sat silent, unwilling to oppose her.

Wounded but not weeping, Dhruva went to his mother Sunīti and told her what had passed. She did not fill his heart with bitterness, nor urge him to fight for what was his; instead she spoke the one truth that would change his destiny. She told him not to seek love where it was not given, and not to ask of men what only the Supreme could grant — for if he desired something truly great, he should seek it from Sriman Nārāyaṇa, who never turns away those who come to Him.

Her words kindled a fire in the boy. He left the palace and walked alone into the dense forest, not knowing where the Lord might be found, but certain that he would find Him. As he wandered, the sage Nārada appeared before him and, to test his resolve, told him that even the greatest sages meditate for lifetimes without beholding the Lord, and that he, a mere child, should return home. But Dhruva would not be turned aside. Seeing in the boy a heart that burned with true longing, Nārada relented, and taught him how to meditate.

He told Dhruva to hold in his heart the image of a dark-complexioned Lord, His form like a monsoon cloud, His neck shaped like a conch, His shoulders broad and His waist slender, His lips like coral and His feet like lotuses, bearing in His four hands the conch, discus, mace, and lotus. He sent the boy to the sacred forest of Madhuvana, and gave him the mantra to chant.

There Dhruva began a penance of extraordinary severity. In the first month he ate only fruit, in the second only water, in the third he lived on air alone, his mind fixed unwaveringly upon the Lord. So powerful did his meditation grow that the very balance of the cosmos was disturbed, and the Devas, alarmed, rushed to Sriman Nārāyaṇa, who told them only that the boy sought Him. And so the Lord appeared. As Dhruva opened his eyes there stood before him Sriman Nārāyaṇa, clad in golden silk, smiling with infinite grace.

Overwhelmed, the boy could find no words, until the Lord gently touched His conch to the child's throat and blessed him with speech.

Dhruva, his eyes filled with tears, confessed that he had come seeking a mere throne but now desired nothing but the Lord Himself. Moved by such innocence and devotion, the Lord granted him far more than he had ever asked.

He promised the boy that he would rule the earth in righteousness for many years, and that when his time was done he would ascend beyond the Brahmandam itself, to shine forever as the Dhruva Mandala, an eternal realm untouched by destruction; and his mother Sunīti, who had earned it through her own devotion, would join him there. So the child who had once been denied a place on his father's lap was granted a place beyond the universe — for the one who seeks the Supreme, even beginning with a worldly wish, receives in the end His eternal refuge.
