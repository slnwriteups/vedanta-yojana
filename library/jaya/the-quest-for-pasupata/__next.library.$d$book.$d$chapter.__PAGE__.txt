Bhīma argued that the thirteen years were only a trap to let Duryodhana entrench his power, and even proposed that by certain sacred incantations they might compress the thirteen years into thirteen days and so fulfil the letter of the oath while escaping its cost. But Yudhishthira refused it utterly, holding that truth is higher than power and must be kept whatever it costs, that a vow honoured only in its letter and cheated in its spirit is no honoured vow at all.
But if he would not make war before the time, he would prepare for the war that must come at the end of it; and on the counsel of Vyāsa, he sent Arjuna to the Himālayas to win divine weapons from the gods against the day of battle. And Arjuna climbed the rugged heights of Indrakīla and performed severe austerities. There Indra came to him first, disguised as an old ascetic, and tested him by offering him celestial pleasures in place of weapons; and Arjuna refused, saying his only desire was to protect dharma upon the earth. And then Arjuna turned his penance toward Lord Shiva.
And Shiva came to test him in a stranger way. A wild boar charged at Arjuna, and Arjuna shot it, and at the very same instant a hunter, a Kirāta, also pierced it with a shaft; and a fierce dispute arose over whose kill it was, and it grew into open combat between Arjuna and the hunter. And Arjuna, for all his supreme skill, could not prevail: his arrows were swallowed up, his mighty bow Gāṇḍīva was wrenched from him, and he was overpowered. And then, at last, he understood, and recognized that the hunter was none other than Shiva himself, come to test him; and he fell down in total surrender at the god's feet. And Shiva, well pleased with Arjuna's courage and his humility both, raised him up, restored his bow, and gave him the greatest of gifts, the infallible Pāshupata weapon, the ultimate astra, against the day of the war. Agastya, the story went, once saw his own ancestors hanging head-downward in a void, suffering because he had begotten no children to continue their line and secure their peace; and so, to fulfil his duty to them, he resolved to marry, and wedded the beautiful princess Lopāmudrā of Vidarbha. But Lopāmudrā, raised in a palace, found the bare poverty of his forest hermitage hard, and asked that before they had children they might live for a time in royal comfort, in the manner she was used to. And Agastya, unwilling to spend his hard-won spiritual merit on mere temporary wealth, set out instead as a humble beggar to ask riches of the wealthy kings of the earth.
And here the story folds in a quiet lesson in governance. For as Agastya examined the treasuries of king after king, he found in each that the state's expenditure exactly equalled its revenue, that there was no true surplus anywhere, and that to take a gift from any of these kings would only mean laying a heavier tax on their common people to make up the loss. And so he would not take from them, for he would not enrich himself at the cost of the poor; a lesson, the tellers note, in what righteous rule requires, that a king's wealth is his people's wealth, and not to be given away to enrich one man at the expense of the many.
Agastya then came to a demon named Ilvala, who had a cruel custom of murdering brāhmaṇas: he would turn his brother Vātāpi into a goat, cook and serve the meat to his guest, and then call Vātāpi to come forth, and the demon would tear his way out of the guest's belly and kill him.
