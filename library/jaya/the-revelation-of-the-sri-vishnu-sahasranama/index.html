JAYA: A Journey of the Mahabharata

The Revelation of the Vishṇu Sahasranāma

At the height of the Shāntiparva, in the silence of the Kurukshetra field, Yudhishthira sat at the feet of the dying Bhīshma, with Krishna and Vyāsa and the assembled sages as witnesses to this supreme handing-down of spiritual inheritance. And seeking a path that all humanity could follow through the dark age of Kali that was coming, Yudhishthira put six great questions to the grandsire: Who is the one supreme Deity over all the world? What is the highest goal of human life?

By praising and worshipping whom will a person attain prosperity here and bliss hereafter? By meditating on whom is the same attained? Of all the paths of dharma, which is the best and surest means to grace? And by reciting what will a mortal be freed from the bondage of birth and death?

And Bhīshma answered, decisively, that the continuous, loving chanting of the thousand names of the Lord is the supreme and most accessible path of devotion. For unlike the elaborate fire-sacrifices, which require particular qualifications and times and places and materials, and are open only to some, this practice of loving remembrance is wholly universal, free of every external constraint of purity or caste or station or place, requiring only sincerity and devotion of the heart.

And so Bhīshma spoke forth the thousand names, each a distinct auspicious attribute of the Divine, and the sage Vyāsa strung them together as they flowed from the dying patriarch's lips, and preserved them for all time as the Vishṇu Sahasranāma, the Thousand Names.

Let us mark the beauty of what is offered here, at the deathbed of the grandsire, as the crown of the whole book's teaching. Yudhishthira asks for the surest path to the Divine, expecting perhaps some difficult discipline reserved for the qualified few; and Bhīshma gives him instead the most democratic answer imaginable: that the highest path is simply the loving remembrance of the Lord's names, open to everyone, high and low, learned and simple, requiring nothing but a sincere and devoted heart.

This is the same truth that has run through the whole epic, through Shabari's berries and Draupadī's cry and Krishna's single grain of rice: that the Divine asks not for elaborate qualification but for love, and that the way home is open to all who will walk it with a whole heart.

It is a fitting crown for the dying patriarch to give, and for the whole story to arrive at: that in the coming dark age, when the old disciplines grow too hard for fallen humanity, there remains this one sure and simple and universal road, the loving remembrance of the Divine, which no one is too low or too unlearned to travel.
