JAYA: A Journey of the Mahabharata

Kacha's Devotion and the Curse of Sanjīvinī

In the ancient days there was an unending war between the gods and the demons, the Devas and the Asuras, for the lordship of the three worlds, and in that long war the demons held one terrible advantage, and the story of how the gods overcame it is a story of devotion, of cunning, and of a curse born from a rejected love.

The advantage was this. Each side had its great preceptor, its spiritual master: Brihaspati guided the gods, and Shukrāchārya guided the demons. But Shukrāchārya alone, of all the beings in the three worlds, knew the secret of Sanjīvinī, a sacred mantra that could call the dead back to life. And so, in the war, whenever a demon warrior fell, Shukrāchārya raised him up again to fight, while the slain gods stayed dead; and no army can win against a foe whose dead will not stay dead.

The gods were losing, and losing not for want of courage but for want of that one secret, and they saw that unless they could learn it, the demons would grind them down in the end.

So the gods went to Kacha, the noble son of their own preceptor Brihaspati, and laid before him a hard and dangerous mission. Go to Shukrāchārya, they begged him; become his student; win his trust; and by some means learn from him the secret of Sanjīvinī, so that we too may raise our dead and match the demons. It was a perilous errand, for it meant living among the enemy, in the demon capital, in the very house of the demons' master; but Kacha understood that his duty to the common good outweighed his own safety, and he accepted.

He went to Shukrāchārya and asked, with all humility, to be received as a student, naming his noble lineage; and by the sacred law of the sages, a worthy student who comes seeking knowledge cannot be turned away, and Shukrāchārya, pleased with the young man's bearing and his descent, took him in. And Kacha served him for years, faithfully, flawlessly, the perfect student.

Now Shukrāchārya had a daughter, Devayānī, spirited and lovely, whom he cherished; and Kacha, part by design and part by the natural sweetness of his nature, devoted himself to pleasing her, with song and dance and gentle companionship, and won her deep affection, all the while keeping strictly his vows of chastity.

But the demons were not fools, and they grew suspicious. They guessed why Kacha had truly come, that he meant to carry off the secret of Sanjīvinī to the enemy, and they resolved to kill him before he could succeed. And here begins the strange and terrible sequence at the heart of the tale. One day, while Kacha was out grazing his master's cattle, the demons fell upon him, killed him, tore his body to pieces, and fed the pieces to wild dogs.

When the cattle came home without him, Devayānī was frantic, and ran to her father weeping that Kacha had not returned and that she could not live without him. And Shukrāchārya, unable to bear his daughter's grief, used the Sanjīvinī art and called Kacha back, and the young man's scattered body reassembled and he returned, alive, smiling, telling how the demons had killed him though he knew not how he had been restored.

So the demons killed him a second time, and this time ground his body to paste and mixed it into the sea; and again Devayānī wept, and again Shukrāchārya raised him. And then the demons devised a cleverer murder. They killed Kacha a third time, burned his body to ashes, and mixed the ashes secretly into the wine that Shukrāchārya loved to drink, so that the master himself unknowingly swallowed his own student. And when Kacha did not return, Devayānī, in despair, declared that she would fast to death and follow him into the fire.

Once more, to comfort his daughter, Shukrāchārya used the Sanjīvinī art and called Kacha to appear; and to his astonishment, he heard the young man's voice answering from within his own belly. Kacha explained: that he had been burned, his ashes mixed in the wine, and that he was now trapped inside his master's body. And here Shukrāchārya faced a dreadful dilemma, and it is the pivot of the story. For Kacha to live, he must tear his way out of Shukrāchārya's stomach; but that would kill Shukrāchārya.

The student's life could be bought only with the master's death.

And Shukrāchārya found a solution worthy of his wisdom, and it turned upon the very secret at the heart of the whole tale. He spoke to Kacha within him: You have won your goal, son of Brihaspati; I will teach you the Sanjīvinī mantra now, this moment, while you are inside me. Then, when you tear your way out and I fall dead, you must at once use the mantra to raise me, so that my daughter need grieve for neither of us.

And so, from inside his own body, the master taught the student the very secret the student had come to steal; and Kacha, having learned it, tore his way out of Shukrāchārya, emerging like the full moon from a dark cloud, while the great preceptor fell dead upon the grass; and Kacha at once chanted the mantra he had just been given, and Shukrāchārya rose, alive and whole.

The student had won the secret; the master had been raised by his own art in the hands of another; and the demons' cruel murders had, in the end, only served to place the secret exactly where they most feared it would go.

Shukrāchārya, sobered by the deceit that had been worked through his own cup of wine, made from it a solemn warning for all people ever after: that virtue deserts the man who, through folly, drinks wine and loses his wits, and that such a one becomes an object of scorn; let this, he said, be held as a binding rule. So even the master's humiliation was turned to a teaching.

Kacha stayed on to finish his studies, and when at last his term was complete and he prepared to return to the world of the gods, Devayānī came to him and spoke, with the frank dignity of a noble woman, what was in her heart. You have won my love, she told him; I have loved you long; now marry me and make me happy. And here Kacha had to refuse her, and his refusal, gentle but firm, was rooted in dharma, and it cost him dearly. You are my master's daughter, he told her, ever worthy of my reverence.

And more than that: because I was, in a manner, born again out of your father's body, you are as a sister to me, and a brother may not wed his sister. It would be against all law. Forgive me, and let me go.

And Devayānī, wounded in her love and her pride together, answered his refusal with a curse: that the Sanjīvinī mantra he had won from her father should prove fruitless to him in his own hour of need. And Kacha accepted the curse calmly, and answered it with the deepest thing in him. I accept your curse, Devayānī, he said. Though I myself may never use this knowledge, I will teach it to others, and they shall conquer death; the knowledge will not be lost, only my own use of it.

For I am bound to the path of dharma, which is greater than my own life and greater than my own gain. And with those words he departed for the heavens, leaving behind him a curse he had accepted without bitterness and a devotion to duty that outweighed even the power over death itself.

There is a lesson in Kacha to carry forward, and it is a quiet one. He had gained the power over death, the very thing he had risked everything to win; and he gave it away, freely, to others, keeping none of its use for himself, because a curse had bound it and because dharma mattered to him more than his own advantage. That is the mark of the truly noble soul, that it holds even its greatest prize with an open hand, and would rather serve the good with a gift it cannot use itself than betray the good to keep the gift.

Kacha lost the use of his hard-won knowledge and did not count it a loss, because he had never sought it for himself.
