5:T9c0,The curse that Bharadvāja had laid upon Raibhya now worked itself out, and its working carries the story's judgment on the difference between learning and virtue.
Raibhya had two sons, Parāvasu and Arvāvasu, both deeply learned scholars of the Vedas. One night, as Parāvasu walked through the dark forest to attend a great sacrifice being held by King Brihadyumna, he saw a shape moving in the bushes, and took it for a wild beast, and shot it; and found that he had killed his own father, Raibhya, who was walking there wrapped in a deerskin. So the curse was fulfilled, the father slain by the eldest son exactly as Bharadvāja had decreed.
And now the true test came, the test not of learning but of character, and it exposed the two brothers utterly. Parāvasu, terrified of the guilt of having killed his father, begged his younger brother Arvāvasu to take upon himself the long penances of expiation for the crime, while Parāvasu himself completed the king's sacrifice; and Arvāvasu, out of pure brotherly love, agreed, and spent years in the rigorous rites of purification for a sin that was not his. But when Arvāvasu returned at last to the sacrificial hall to rejoin his brother, Parāvasu, corrupted by jealousy and by the wish to be rid of him, loudly proclaimed before all that Arvāvasu was a sinner, a man guilty of killing his own father, unfit to enter the holy ground. And the guards, believing him, drove the innocent Arvāvasu away.
But Arvāvasu retired to the forest and performed intense penances to the Sun God, who in time appeared and vindicated him and declared his innocence before all. And the story sets the two brothers side by side and draws its plain lesson: that scriptural learning is only an outer garment unless it has been digested into lived and active virtue. Both brothers knew the Vedas equally; but Parāvasu, for all his learning, lacked the basic purity of heart, and used his knowledge to shield his own guilt and to slander his innocent brother; while the humble Arvāvasu lived the true spirit of dharma, taking his brother's guilt upon himself in love and bearing slander without bitterness. Learning had made Parāvasu clever and left him corrupt; virtue had made Arvāvasu great. और अब सच्ची परीक्षा आई, विद्या की नहीं बल्कि चरित्र की परीक्षा, और उसने दोनों भाइयों को पूर्णतः उघाड़ दिया। पराव्सु, अपने पिता को मारने के अपराधबोध से भयभीत होकर, अपने छोटे भाई अर्वावसु से विनती की कि वह उस अपराध के प्रायश्चित की लंबी तपस्या स्वयं ले ले, जबकि पराव्सु स्वयं राजा का यज्ञ पूरा करे; और अर्वावसु ने, शुद्ध भ्रातृ-प्रेम से, स्वीकार किया, और एक ऐसे पाप के लिए वर्षों तक कठोर शुद्धिकरण अनुष्ठानों में बिताए जो उसका नहीं था। परंतु जब अर्वावसु अंततः अपने भाई से पुनः मिलने यज्ञभवन लौटा, पराव्सु, ईर्ष्या और उससे छुटकारा पाने की इच्छा से भ्रष्ट होकर, सबके सामने ऊँचे स्वर में घोषित किया कि अर्वावसु एक पापी है, अपने ही पिता की हत्या का दोषी, पवित्र स्थान में प्रवेश के अयोग्य। और रक्षकों ने, उस पर विश्वास करके, निर्दोष अर्वावसु को खदेड़ दिया।

Ashtāvakra had been born with eight deformities twisting his body, the fruit of a curse his own scholarly father Kahoḍa had laid upon him when, still in the womb, the unborn child had presumed to correct his father's faulty recitation of the Vedas. So Ashtāvakra came into the world bent and broken in body; but within that broken frame lived an intellect of peerless clarity, a mind that was a perfect mirror of the highest wisdom.
At the age of twelve, Ashtāvakra journeyed to the court of King Janaka to challenge the proud court scholar Vandin, who had defeated many learned men in debate and drowned the losers in the ocean, and among them Ashtāvakra's own father. And as the boy approached, the king's men commanded the deformed child to make way; and Ashtāvakra rebuked them with a dignity beyond his years: even a king must make way for the deformed, the blind, for women, and for those who carry the light of the Vedas. And when the assembled scholars of the court laughed at his twisted limbs, Ashtāvakra answered them with words that cut to the very heart of the matter: I thought this was an assembly of scholars, but I see it is a gathering of cobblers, who judge a man by his skin rather than by his soul.
And then, in a profound debate on the nature of the One and the Many, Ashtāvakra silenced the mighty Vandin, proving his absolute mastery of the deepest philosophy; and Vandin, defeated, revealed himself as the son of the god Varuṇa, and restored to life all the scholars he had drowned. So the boy triumphed, and won back his father, and the whole story stands as a rebuke to the shallow judgment of the world. For the world saw a broken and deformed body and assumed a lesser soul; and the soul within was greater than any straight-limbed scholar in that hall. The eternal self, the story tells us, is wholly unaffected by the shape or the soundness of the body it wears; the body is a vehicle, and a deformed vehicle may carry a perfect soul, as a plain vessel may hold the finest wine.
