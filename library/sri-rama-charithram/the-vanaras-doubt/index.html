Sri Rama Charithram

The Vānaras’ Doubt

Vibhīshaṇa came across the sea and appeared above the vānara camp, seeking Rāma, and his coming threw the camp into a sharp debate, for here was a rākshasa, a demon, a brother of Rāvaṇa himself, coming from the enemy’s city to the very heart of Rāma’s host, and the question of whether to receive him or to destroy him was not a simple one, and the arguments on both sides reveal the differing wisdoms of Rāma’s counsellors.

For most of the vānara leaders, seeing a demon approach from Lankā, counselled suspicion and rejection. Sugrīva above all was wary, and his reasoning was the natural reasoning of caution in war. This is a demon, he warned, a rākshasa, and worse, the very brother of our enemy Rāvaṇa. Why should such a one come to us now, on the eve of battle, if not as a spy, a deceiver, an agent sent by Rāvaṇa to worm his way into our councils, to learn our plans, to betray us at the crucial hour?

It is a trick, surely; it is too convenient; the enemy’s own brother does not simply defect to us out of love of righteousness on the very eve of war. Do not trust him. Do not receive him into our camp and our confidence. Send him away, or destroy him, but do not open our councils to one who is by blood and birth our enemy and who comes to us with a story too good to be true.

So Sugrīva counselled, and many of the leaders agreed, and their caution was reasonable, for in war a defector from the enemy’s own royal house, arriving just before battle, is exactly what a spy would pretend to be.

And Lakshmaṇa, too, we may imagine, inclined to caution, for it was ever his instinct to suspect a threat to his brother and to guard against it, and the arrival of a demon from the enemy’s city was exactly the kind of danger his protective wariness would fear.

But not all counselled suspicion, and it was Hanumān, characteristically, who spoke for the other side, and his counsel showed the discernment that set him apart. Hanumān had been to Lankā; he had seen its court; he had, perhaps, marked Vibhīshaṇa there, the one righteous voice in that hall of evil. And Hanumān judged not by the outward fact of Vibhīshaṇa’s birth and kinship but by the inward evidence of his bearing and his words. Consider him truly, Hanumān urged. Look at his face, his manner, his speech.

A spy comes with guile, with a false and slippery bearing, with the marks of deceit upon him. This one comes openly, in evident sincerity, with the bearing of a man who has made a hard and honest choice and staked everything upon it. And consider his story: is it truly so incredible? We know that Vibhīshaṇa counselled against the crime; we know there was one righteous voice in Rāvaṇa’s court; is it so strange that such a one, seeing his brother rush toward ruin and being spurned for his wisdom, should leave, and come to the side of right?

His defection is not too good to be true; it is exactly what a righteous soul in his position would do. I judge him sincere. I say receive him. And even if there were some risk, consider what it would mean to turn away one who comes seeking refuge and righteousness; and consider, too, how much such a one could aid us, for who knows Lankā and Rāvaṇa and all their secrets better than Rāvaṇa’s own brother? To receive him is both the righteous course and the wise one.

So the debate went back and forth, suspicion against discernment, the caution of war against the reading of the heart, and it came at last to Rāma, whose judgment would decide, and Rāma’s decision, and the principle on which he made it, is the true subject of this chapter and the doorway to the great teaching that follows.

For Rāma listened to all the counsel, the wary and the trusting both, and then he decided, and he decided to receive Vibhīshaṇa, and he decided it on a ground higher than mere calculation of risk, on a ground of principle that would open, in the next chapter, into one of the deepest teachings of the whole epic.

We should mark the character of the debate before we pass to Rāma’s decision, for it is instructive. Both sides had reason. Sugrīva’s caution was not folly; in the cold calculus of war, a defector from the enemy’s royal house on the eve of battle is a genuine risk, and to receive him is to gamble. Hanumān’s discernment was not naivety; he judged by real evidence, the bearing and the words and the plausible story of the man, and read the heart rightly where mere suspicion would have read it wrong.

The debate is a genuine one, between two real goods, prudent caution and open-hearted discernment, and the story does not pretend that either is simply foolish. But it does show us that Hanumān’s discernment saw truer than Sugrīva’s caution, that the reading of the heart reached a truth that the calculus of risk would have missed, and that there are times when to judge a soul by its outward category, demon, enemy, brother of the foe, is to miss entirely the truth of what it is and what it has chosen to become.

And this is the doorway to Rāma’s decision, for Rāma would not judge Vibhīshaṇa by his birth as a demon or his kinship to the enemy, but by the choice he had made and the refuge he now sought; and in refusing to turn away one who came seeking refuge, whatever his origin, Rāma was about to articulate the great principle of sharaṇāgati, of surrender and grace, the doctrine that whoever comes seeking refuge with a sincere heart shall be received and protected, whatever they have been, whoever they are, without exception and without limit.

The vānaras’ doubt, and the debate it provoked, set the stage for Rāma to declare this doctrine in its fullness, and so the suspicion of the camp became the occasion for one of the most luminous of all Rāma’s teachings, the teaching that divine grace turns none away, and that the one who truly seeks refuge is received, though he come from the very household of the enemy, though he have been the darkest of beings, if only he comes sincerely, saying, I am yours.
