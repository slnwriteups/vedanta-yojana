Sri Rama Charithram

Kaikeyī Succumbs

Dasharatha came to Kaikeyī’s apartments that night in the best mood of his long life. Of all his queens she had always been the one whose company most lifted him, the one whose rooms he sought when he wished simply to be happy, and on this night of all nights, with the coronation of his beloved son arranged for the morning and the whole city singing outside, he came to share his joy with the wife who had always known best how to share it. He came expecting light, and music, and Kaikeyī bright and glad to meet him.

He found darkness.

The chamber was unlit. And when his eyes adjusted he saw, with a shock that stopped him in the doorway, his queen lying on the bare floor, her jewels cast aside, her hair unbound and disordered, her fine clothes exchanged for the soiled garments of mourning. This was the kopa-griha, the sulking-room, and its meaning was unmistakable: a queen displayed herself so only in the extremity of grief or anger, to compel a husband’s attention to some wound. Dasharatha’s happiness turned to alarm in a heartbeat.

That Kaikeyī, his Kaikeyī, of all of them the least given to sulks and scenes, should be lying so on this night of all nights, filled him with a bewildered dread.

He went to her at once, and knelt, and gathered her up, all tenderness and fear. What was this? Who had wronged her? Was she ill? Had someone offended her? Only name it, he said, and it would be set right; there was nothing in his power he would not do to lift this grief from her; let her only tell him what she wished and it was hers. He heaped reassurances on her, this doting old husband, promising the earth to comfort the wife he loved best, entirely unaware that he was, with every fond word, tightening the noose around his own neck.

For Kaikeyī let him make his promises, and then she asked him to make them binding. She would tell him her wish, she said, but first he must swear. He must swear by all that a king holds sacred, by his own truth, by the honour of the house of Ikshvāku, that he would grant what she asked, whatever it might be, without refusing. Bind yourself, she said. Give me your oath, and then you shall hear it.

And Dasharatha, besotted, suspecting nothing, laughing almost at her earnestness, swore. He swore the great oath. He called his own truth and his lineage and his gods to witness that whatever Kaikeyī asked, he would perform it, and that he would sooner die than break his word to her. He gave her, freely and completely, the one thing that would leave him no way out. And having sworn, he asked her, gently, what she wished.

Then Kaikeyī rose, and the woman who spoke was not the woman Dasharatha had loved for all those years. Her voice was cold and level and certain. She reminded him of the old battlefield, and the two boons he had granted her and she had never claimed. Now, she said, she would claim them, and he was bound by his oath just now sworn to grant them, and she named them.

The first: that Bharata, her son, should be crowned Yuvarāja in the morning, in the place prepared for Rāma.

The second: that Rāma should be sent that same day into the Daṇḍaka forest, clad in bark, to live as an ascetic for fourteen years.

For a moment Dasharatha did not understand. The words were too monstrous to land all at once. He asked her, surely, to repeat them, or perhaps he simply stared, his mind refusing the sense of what his ears had taken in. And then it reached him, the whole shape of it, and it struck him down as no weapon on any battlefield ever had. He fell.

The tellers say he collapsed as a great tree falls, all at once, the strength going out of him, and lay stunned on the floor of the chamber where he had come to be happy, and could not speak, and could not rise.

Let us stop and see clearly what has been done here, because the horror of it is precise and worth understanding. Kaikeyī has not asked for a crime exactly. She has asked for two things that are, in the bare letter, within a king’s power to grant, the coronation of one son, the sending-away of another, and she has secured them by the one mechanism a man of honour cannot escape: his own given word. That is the trap’s cruel elegance.

Dasharatha could refuse a demand backed by force; he could argue against a demand backed by mere will; but a demand backed by his own sacred oath, freely sworn moments before, he cannot refuse without destroying the very thing that makes him who he is. To break the oath is to become a king whose word is worthless, to poison the well of truth on which his whole kingdom and his whole honour rest. Kaikeyī, or rather the poison speaking through Kaikeyī, has weaponized his virtue against him.

His greatest strength, his absolute fidelity to his word, has been turned into the instrument of his destruction. There is no crueler trap than the one built out of a good man’s own goodness.

And there is a further cruelty in it, quieter and sadder. The old king, lying broken on the floor, understood that the wife he had trusted above all others had planned this, had lain in wait for him in the dark with this ready, had let him pour out his love and make his fond promises knowing all the while what she would ask. The betrayal was not only of his kingdom and his son but of the intimate trust of decades, and it went into him like a cold blade.

The Kaikeyī he had loved was, as far as he could tell, simply gone, and a stranger with her face sat over him demanding the ruin of everything he held dear.

When he could speak, he did not command; he had no ground to command, for he had sworn. He pleaded. He begged. This too is part of the terrible scene, that a great king was reduced to weeping at his wife’s feet, offering her anything, everything, if only she would release him from these two demands. Ask for the whole earth for Bharata, he said in effect, ask for treasure without limit, ask for anything under heaven, but not this, not the exile of Rāma, not the breaking of my heart and my kingdom.

He wept and he clutched at her and he abased himself utterly, an old man destroyed, and it moved her not at all.

For Kaikeyī had made herself cold, and she turned his own honour back upon him with every plea. A king’s word cannot be broken, she said. You have sworn. If you refuse me now, you are forsworn before your court and your gods, and the house of Ikshvāku, which has never in all its generations broken a promise, is disgraced forever through you. Would you save your son and damn your honour? Would you have it said that Dasharatha lied? She held his virtue up before him like a mirror and made it a cage.

He had built his whole life on the sanctity of his given word, and she used that against him with a precision that was almost surgical, so that the more he loved his honour the more surely he was bound to destroy his heart.

The night wore on toward morning, the lamps of the coronation still burning across the city, the people still asleep in their happy certainty, and in the dark chamber the old king lay broken between two impossible things: his love for his son and his fidelity to his word, and Kaikeyī immovable above him insisting he could not keep both. He wept until he had no more strength to weep. He pleaded until pleading was plainly useless. And still the demands stood, unwithdrawn, unwithdrawable, waiting for the daylight that would make them real.

He was still lying there, unmanned, unmoving, when the first grey of the dreadful morning began to show, and with it came the sounds of the city stirring toward the celebration that would never happen, and the knowledge that soon, very soon, someone would have to send for Rāma.

Wife or Demon?

The night would not release him. Dasharatha lay on the floor of the dark chamber where he had come to be happy, and the hours crawled by, and the demands did not soften, and slowly, as a man drowning in slow water understands at last that no one is coming, the old king began to grasp that this was real, that it would not be taken back, that the morning was coming and would make it true.

He passed through every stage a broken man passes through. First there had been disbelief, the mind simply refusing the words. Then there had been pleading, the frantic offering of everything else, the whole earth for Bharata, treasure without measure, anything, anything but this.

And when the pleading struck the cold wall of Kaikeyī’s resolve and fell back useless, he passed into something worse, into a grief so total that it took the shape almost of madness, and he wept and raved and clutched at her feet and at his own breast, and called on the dawn not to come, and it did him no good at all.

And then, when grief had exhausted itself, came anger, and for the first time in all the years of their marriage Dasharatha looked at Kaikeyī with something other than love. He saw her now as though for the first time, this cold implacable creature sitting above his ruin, and he did not know her. Where was the woman he had cherished? Where was the wife whose company had been the joy of his old age, who had loved his son Rāma with an open heart only yesterday?

The thing that sat before him now, unmoved by his tears, weaponizing his own honour against him, deaf to every appeal of love or pity, seemed to him no wife at all.

Are you a woman, he cried out to her at last, or are you some demon sent to destroy my house? For no woman, no human wife, could do this thing. What creature but a rākshasī, a she-demon out of the pit, could sit unmoved while an old man wept at her feet and beg for nothing but the ruin of the best and gentlest of sons?

He had shared his throne with her, his bed, his whole heart; he had denied her nothing in all their years; and she had waited, patient as a serpent, for the one night when she could strike the blow that would kill him and destroy his line and cast his beloved son into the wilderness. This was not the act of a wife. It was the act of an enemy who had lived in his house wearing a wife’s face.

Let us pause on that terrible question, wife or demon, because the story means us to sit with it, and because the honest answer is more disturbing than either simple choice. Kaikeyī was not, in truth, a demon. She was a woman, a good woman once, who had been poisoned.

And that is the more frightening truth, for a demon is a thing apart, a horror we can hold at arm’s length, but a good soul turned to this by nothing more than jealous fear and a bitter whisper, that is a horror that lives close to home, that could visit any house, that reminds us how thin the wall is between the love that would give everything and the fear that would destroy everything, and how quickly one can be turned into the other by the patient dripping of poison into a frightened heart.

Dasharatha called her a demon because he could not bear the truer thing: that the wife he loved had really done this, as herself, with her own will, having been made capable of it by an evil that worked not from outside her but from within, through her own real fears turned against her.

And Kaikeyī heard herself called a demon, and it did not move her, and that failure to be moved is itself the measure of how deep the poison had gone. She answered him, and her answers were all of one kind: she threw his own virtue back in his face. You gave your word, she said. You swore the great oath. The house of Ikshvāku has never broken a promise in all its generations. Will you be the first? Will you have it said forever that Dasharatha lied, that his word was smoke, that a king of the Solar Line broke faith to spare himself a grief?

She did not argue that the exile was just. She did not need to. She had only to remind him, again and again, remorselessly, that he was bound, that his honour was the chain, that to save his son he must become the one thing he had spent his whole life refusing to be: a man forsworn.

It was a devil’s logic and it was airtight, and the old king had no answer to it, because in a terrible way it was true. He had built his life on the sanctity of the given word. His people trusted him because his word was iron. His whole moral world rested on the principle that a king does not lie, that a promise is a promise though it cost the promiser everything. And now that very principle, the foundation of all his honour, had become the trap that would force him to destroy his own heart. If he broke his word, he betrayed everything he was.

If he kept it, he betrayed everyone he loved. There was no third road. Kaikeyī had built a cage out of his own goodness, and every bar of it was something he himself believed in, and so he could not break a single one.

He raged, and he wept, and he cursed her, and he cursed the hour he had granted the boons, and he cursed the fate that had brought him to this, and none of it changed anything. The demands stood. His oath stood. And the daylight came anyway, grey and pitiless, as daylight does, indifferent to the ruin it was about to expose.

The old king lay spent. He had no more anger and no more tears. He understood now, in the grey light, that he was going to lose his son, and that he was going to lose him by his own hand, through his own honoured word, and that there was nothing, nothing at all, that he could do to prevent it. The knowledge hollowed him out.

Whatever had been strong in him, the warrior who fought beside the gods, the king who ruled a golden age, was gone, and what was left was only a heartbroken old man who could not even rise from the floor, who could not bear to give the order that must be given, and who lay in the growing light dreading the moment when someone would have to send for Rāma and tell him what his father’s oath had done to him.

He could not do it. He had not the strength even to speak the words. And so it fell to Kaikeyī, cold and efficient to the end, to send for Rāma herself, to summon the son to the chamber where the father lay broken, so that she could deliver with her own mouth the decree that the father had no strength left to speak. She gave the order. A servant went.

And somewhere across the waking palace, in the rooms where he had spent the night in the sacred vigil of one about to be crowned, Rāma received the summons to attend his father, and rose, and came, expecting, perhaps, that this was the beginning of his coronation, and walked toward the darkened chamber where the worst news of his life was waiting.

Behold a Wonder!

Rāma came to the chamber expecting joy and found catastrophe.

He had risen that morning from the vigil of one about to be crowned, purified, fasting, his mind turned toward the great responsibility he was to take up. When the summons came to attend his father, he thought nothing strange in it; the coronation was that very day; of course the king would wish to see him. He came, as he always came to his father, with love and with reverence, ready to kneel and receive a blessing.

He crossed the threshold and stopped.

The room was dark and disordered. His father, the great king, the conqueror, the mighty Dasharatha, lay collapsed and weeping upon the floor, unable to rise, unable even to look up, so unmanned by grief that he could not speak his son’s name. And beside him stood Kaikeyī, upright, cold, dry-eyed, with a face Rāma had never seen her wear. In an instant Rāma understood that something terrible had happened, though he could not yet guess what. His first thought, his only thought, was for his father.

He went to him at once, all alarm and tenderness, kneeling beside the broken old man, taking his hand, asking what was wrong, who had wounded him, how he might set it right. Had he, Rāma, given some offence? Only let him know the cause of his father’s grief and he would spend his life to cure it.

But Dasharatha could not answer. He could only weep, and turn his face away, and clutch his son’s hand, and be unable to say the thing that had to be said. The sight of Rāma, come in innocence and love, expecting a crown, about to be told of his exile, was more than the old king could bear, and he broke down entirely and could not speak a word.

So Rāma turned to Kaikeyī, whom he trusted, whom he loved as a second mother, and asked her what had happened to his father, and begged her to tell him plainly, whatever it was.

And Kaikeyī told him. Coldly, completely, sparing nothing, she laid out the whole of it: the two boons, long ago granted; her claiming of them now; the king bound by his oath and unable to refuse. Bharata was to be crowned in Rāma’s place. And Rāma himself was to go that very day into the Daṇḍaka forest, in the bark garments of an ascetic, to live in exile for fourteen years.

The king could not bring himself to speak these commands, she said, because he was weak with grief; but the commands were given, and bound by the royal word, and Rāma, if he was the son of truth he claimed to be, would honour them.

There was a silence in the chamber.

And then Rāma did the thing that is the whole heart of this chapter, the thing that gives it its name, for it was a wonder, and the tellers have marvelled at it ever since. He did not rage. He did not weep. He did not so much as change countenance. He heard the sentence that turned his crown into exile, that took from him in a single morning the kingdom, the throne, the honour, the whole bright future that had been his an hour before, and he received it with perfect, unshaken calm, as serenely as if she had told him some small ordinary thing.

Is that all, he said in effect. Then it is easily done. If my father has given his word, his word must be kept, and I am glad to be the means of keeping it. A son’s first duty is to preserve his father’s honour and to make his father’s word true. If my going to the forest for fourteen years is the price of my father’s truth, then I go gladly, and count it no hardship but a privilege. Let Bharata be crowned; he is worthy, and I shall be glad to see it. Send word to the forest and I will leave today.

Let us stop and understand what we are seeing, because it is easy to read this calm as coldness and it is the opposite of coldness. Rāma was not a man without feeling; he loved the world he was losing as much as any man could. Nor was his calm a matter of not yet understanding; he understood completely, at once, all that was being taken from him. His serenity came from somewhere deeper than feeling and deeper than shock.

It came from a settled conviction, held so far down in him that it had become the very floor he stood on, that duty is the one thing a man truly possesses, the one thing that cannot be taken from him, because it is the one thing he can choose to give. They could take his crown. They could take his kingdom. They could send him into the wilderness.

But whether he met that loss with honour or with bitterness, whether he kept faith with his father’s word or broke under the injustice of it, that was his alone to decide, and no boon and no queen and no cruelty could touch it. In the very moment of his greatest loss, Rāma reached for the one thing no one could take, and held it, and was free.

This is the secret of his calm, and it is worth carrying out of this chamber into our own smaller trials. When everything is taken from us, one thing remains: how we bear it. That remaining thing is small, and it is everything. Rāma knew it, and stood on it, and so the sentence that would have destroyed a lesser man left him untouched in the only place that finally matters.

And so, having accepted his exile without a murmur, Rāma turned his whole attention to the one being in that room who was suffering more than he was: his father. For the old king was destroyed, and Rāma’s calm, far from wounding him, only deepened his agony, because it showed him the full measure of the son he was losing. Rāma knelt again beside Dasharatha and gave himself entirely to consoling him. Do not grieve, father, he said gently. You have done no wrong. You have kept your word, and that is your glory, not your shame.

I am not being punished; I am being given the chance to serve your truth. Fourteen years is not long. I will keep my discipline in the forest and return to you, and these years will pass like a dream, and you must not let this grief consume you. Be at peace. Your honour is safe, and your son is content, and there is nothing here to weep for.

He comforted the man whose oath had ruined him. He soothed the author of his exile. He asked nothing for himself, expressed no reproach, sought no comfort, but poured out his whole strength in the effort to ease his father’s pain.

And the old king clung to him and wept the harder, because every gentle word showed him more clearly the goodness he was casting away, and because he understood now, past all doubt, that the son he was losing was better than any crown, better than any kingdom, better by far than the wretched honour that was forcing him to let him go.

That was the wonder of that dark morning: not that a prince lost a kingdom, for kingdoms are lost every age, but that a man could lose everything in an hour and stand so completely unshaken, and turn from his own ruin to comfort the one who had ruined him. Ayodhyā would remember that morning forever, and would tell it forever, not as the story of an injustice, though it was one, but as the first great showing of what manner of man their Rāma was, and why the love they bore him was not misplaced.
